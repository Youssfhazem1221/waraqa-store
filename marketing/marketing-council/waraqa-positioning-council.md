# Waraqa (ورقة) — Positioning Council Session

**Prepared:** 2026-08-20

## The question before the council

Should Waraqa position and market itself narrowly around the "serious sketcher who's been burned by cheap paper bleeding through" — the framing the competitor-comparison content already assumes — or should it pursue broader mass-availability marketing aimed at anyone in Egypt who might buy a notebook? And how much should the handmade/price story carry that positioning versus simple reach and recall?

**What's at stake:** Waraqa is pre-launch/early-stage — no sales, traffic, or customer data exists yet (confirmed across the SEO, pricing, and content-strategy audits). Whatever positioning gets chosen now will shape the ad copy, content calendar, and shop-page framing before any of it can be tested against real behavior.

## Seated: April Dunford, Byron Sharp, Eugene Schwartz, Rory Sutherland, Seth Godin (council session)

Dunford, Godin, and Schwartz are the strong fits for a positioning/messaging question; Sharp is seated as the designated dissenter against both Dunford's differentiation instinct and Godin's niche-first instinct; Sutherland is added because the entire comparison-content project rests on a price-as-signal bet (charging more than the cheapest marketplace option) that deserves its own scrutiny.

---

### April Dunford — positioning against real alternatives

Dunford's first move is always the same: what would the customer honestly do if Waraqa didn't exist? Here the honest answer isn't "buy a different premium sketchbook brand" — it's "grab whatever's at the nearest stationery counter or add a Jumia listing to an existing cart without checking the gsm." That's the real competitive alternative, and it's mostly indecision and inertia, not a named rival. The comparison content already built gets this right by benchmarking against marketplace generic sketchbooks rather than inventing a boutique competitor that doesn't exist.

From there, the unique attribute is concrete and provable: every one of the 8 SKUs states its gsm and acid-free claim, where marketplace listings are inconsistent brand to brand. The value that attribute enables is confidence — a buyer doesn't have to do gsm math across ten tabs to know their marker won't bleed through. The people who care *a lot* about that value are exactly the ones who've already had a cheap pad fail them mid-drawing — which is a real segment, not a fabricated one, and happens to line up with Godin's instinct below.

Where Dunford would push back on the current draft: the market category framing. Right now the content implicitly competes in "sketchbooks" — a category where price is the only axis buyers know to compare on. Reframing the category as "reliable heavyweight paper for wet media in Egypt" makes the gsm guarantee the obvious point of comparison instead of one spec among many.

**Bottom line:** keep the positioning statement narrow even if the eventual audience reached is broader — position for the customer who cares most, then let distribution widen from there.

### Byron Sharp — distinctiveness and penetration, not differentiation (dissenter)

Sharp's objection starts at the premise: is "the serious ink/marker artist who's been burned by bleed-through" actually a distinct, separable market segment, or is it the same pool of people who also buy the cheap pad for a shopping list on a different day? His duplication-of-purchase law predicts the latter — light and heavy sketchbook buyers overlap far more than brand strategy usually assumes, and a "niche loyal tribe" story is very often a statistical artifact rather than a real segment.

That has a direct implication for the comparison content: narrowing the ad copy and positioning language to "ink/marker/mixed-media artists" risks silently excluding the much larger pool of occasional, casual, gift, or student buyers who would still buy Waraqa if it simply came to mind at the moment they needed a sketchbook — regardless of gsm literacy. Sharp would ask whether the brand is investing in being *easy to think of* across all the situations sketchbook-buying occurs (gift-giving, starting a hobby, replacing a finished pad), or only in winning an argument about paper quality with people already deep in consideration.

He'd also flag a distinctiveness gap independent of the positioning debate: with 8 SKUs sharing a similar kraft-cover, spiral-bound look, would a buyer recognize a Waraqa sketchbook with the logo removed? Building a consistently-used visual asset (a color, a mark, a cover treatment) may matter more for long-run growth than any differentiation claim about gsm.

**Bottom line:** don't mistake a content angle for a market segment — build mental availability broadly, and treat the gsm story as one entry point among several, not the whole positioning.

### Eugene Schwartz — what stage of awareness is this prospect actually in?

Schwartz's first question cuts underneath both Dunford and Sharp: what awareness stage is the real Egyptian sketchbook buyer in? Almost certainly Problem-Aware at best — they've had a drawing smear or a page tear, and they're annoyed — not Product-Aware, since they don't yet know Waraqa exists, and possibly not even that "gsm" is the variable that caused their problem. The market's sophistication stage is also still low: no competitor is making loud, competing claims about paper weight, which means Waraqa doesn't need a new mechanism to win — it can win by simply being the first to state the claim clearly, the way *Breakthrough Advertising*'s stage-one advice prescribes.

This matters concretely for the copy already drafted: the "Waraqa vs. Marketplace Sketchbooks" page's TL;DR leads with a spec comparison (gsm, acid-free) — appropriate for a reader who has already arrived at a comparison page and is Product-Aware or further. But any top-of-funnel material (ads, social posts) aimed at Problem-Aware people should open by channeling the felt frustration — *the smear, the bled-through page, the ruined sketch* — not the spec. Schwartz's method is never to argue the market into caring about gsm from zero; it's to find people who already feel the problem and hand them the word for it.

**Bottom line:** keep the comparison pages spec-forward for comparison-stage readers, but write separate, problem-first copy for anything reaching people earlier in awareness.

### Rory Sutherland — the price is a signal, don't rationalize it away

Sutherland's read on the pricing gap is the opposite of an efficiency problem: a price sitting above the cheapest marketplace pad is exactly the kind of costly signal that makes "handmade in Cairo, inspect before you pay" a credible story rather than a marketing claim. If Waraqa matched Jumia's floor price, the story would stop being believable — cheapness and craft-narrative don't co-exist psychologically, even when the underlying product quality is genuinely comparable.

He'd also flag the COD/WhatsApp confirmation step as a case of the doorman fallacy waiting to happen: it looks like pure friction an efficiency drive would remove, but it may be doing unmeasured work — the manual, human touch is part of what makes "each book is checked by hand" feel true rather than asserted. Before anyone streamlines that flow for cost savings, it's worth asking what belief it's quietly propping up.

On the free-shipping threshold specifically — which the pricing doc recommends simply lowering — Sutherland would want a psychological alternative tried in parallel, not instead: reframe what the fee *is* rather than only changing its number. Calling it a "Courier & Inspection Fee" rather than "Delivery Fee" costs nothing and might change how the same 50 EGP feels, independent of whether the threshold itself also moves.

**Bottom line:** the price premium and the manual COD flow are working as signals, not just costs — don't optimize either one purely on efficiency logic without testing what belief gets lost.

### Seth Godin — is there a tribe here yet, and would they tell anyone?

Godin's question isn't "niche vs. mass," it's "who would tell their friends, and why." Applied here: is anything about Waraqa's actual product remarkable enough to be talked about without an ad budget behind it? The honest answer is probably yes — handmade-in-Cairo, small-batch, inspect-before-you-pay is a genuinely different story than an anonymous marketplace SKU, and that's worth more than reach at this stage, when there's no history and no word-of-mouth yet to spend down.

He'd push toward finding the smallest group who's been burned by bleed-through, delighting them completely, and letting them become the distribution mechanism — rather than buying broad reach for a brand that doesn't have a tribe yet to amplify it. This isn't a rejection of Sharp's eventual point about penetration; it's a sequencing argument — a zero-history brand needs people who care enough to spread it before it can responsibly chase everyone who might, someday, buy a sketchbook.

**Bottom line:** earn the smallest viable audience's advocacy first; treat broad reach as the next phase, not the current one.

## Where the council disagrees

1. **Sharp vs. Dunford/Godin — is "the ink/marker artist" a real segment or a content angle mistaken for one?** Dunford and Godin both build the positioning around a specific, pain-driven buyer; Sharp's duplication-of-purchase law predicts that buyer largely overlaps with the general sketchbook-buying population and that narrowing messaging to them caps reach without truly excluding a different market. **What would settle it:** real purchase data, once it exists — do self-identified "serious" buyers actually behave differently (repeat purchase, higher AOV, specific SKU preference) from casual buyers, or is it the same pool as Sharp predicts?

2. **Schwartz vs. the current draft copy — problem-first or spec-first?** The comparison pages already lead with a spec-based TL;DR; Schwartz's awareness-stage framework says most of the real audience hasn't gotten there yet and needs to be met with the felt problem first. **What would settle it:** A/B test a problem-led headline ("still guessing whether your pen will bleed through?") against the current spec-led opening on top-of-funnel channels, keeping the spec-forward version for the comparison page itself.

3. **Sutherland vs. the pricing doc's straightforward fix — change the number or change the meaning?** The pricing strategy doc recommends lowering the free-shipping threshold outright; Sutherland would rather reframe what the shipping fee signals before touching the number, worried that a purely rational fix leaves psychological value on the table. **What would settle it:** test a reframed fee label against a lowered numeric threshold (and against doing both) and compare conversion — this is cheap to run and doesn't require picking a side in advance.

## Chair's synthesis

Given Waraqa has zero real customer or traffic data yet, the defensible move is to **sequence, not choose once**: position narrow for now (Dunford, Godin) because that's the only version of the story that's genuinely testable with the content already built, while treating Sharp's reach argument as the next phase rather than a rejection of the current one — not a permanent bet on a niche that hasn't been confirmed to exist as a real segment. Concretely:

- **Do:** Ship the comparison pages as drafted (they're already positioned correctly for a comparison-stage reader). Write a separate, problem-first version of the same argument for top-of-funnel/ad copy per Schwartz. Test Sutherland's fee-reframe alongside the pricing doc's threshold change rather than picking one. Hold off on broadening messaging to "anyone who buys a notebook" until there's a tribe to broaden from.
- **Tripwire:** Once real order data exists, check it against Sharp's prediction — if "serious" and "casual" buyers turn out to be the same overlapping pool with no distinct behavior, retire the niche-only framing and shift budget toward broad mental-availability plays rather than deepening a segment that isn't actually separable.
- **Execute with:** `ab-testing` (problem-first vs. spec-first copy; fee-label vs. threshold change), `analytics` (the purchase data needed to resolve the Sharp vs. Dunford/Godin question), `copywriting` (the problem-first top-of-funnel variant Schwartz calls for).
